MANY WAYS TO HEAR A CONNECTION

One collection.
Many kinds of similarity.

Choose a song, choose a lens, and hear what stays similar when the musical context changes.

This experiment adapts Jasper Lu’s Concept Lenses to Music4All’s MuQ-MuLan embeddings. Jasper’s blog post explains prompted similarity subspaces.

How a lens is made

The members of a lens become musical descriptions. MuQ-MuLan embeds those descriptions in the same 512-dimensional joint space as the audio, although the text and audio distributions need not coincide. We center the normalized member vectors, compute their covariance eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and retain enough directions to explain 80% of member variance. Projecting audio into those directions gives a different way to measure similarity.

Control members, such as instrumental music without vocals or recordings without effects, participate with the same weight as other members. They are reference descriptions, not hard filters. The Prompts panel shows every description and marks the controls.

Text or sparse inversion

The text basis uses MuQ-MuLan text embeddings directly. The sparse basis uses the repository’s original Adam inversion with the L0-20 SAE checkpoint, packaged manifold prior, and prior-mean initialization: 500 optimization steps, learning rate 0.001, and prior regularization 0.0001. Each sparse code is decoded into a normalized 512-dimensional vector before the eigenbasis is computed. Audio embeddings remain unchanged.

Search any song

All 109,269 tracks are available as starting songs. The site lazily loads the normalized float32 projected embeddings for the selected fixed lens; a browser worker computes cosine neighbours over the entire corpus. The full-space top ten is precomputed for every song to avoid downloading the raw 512-dimensional corpus. Captions and original text-prompt affinities load in small shards when needed. The static page streams the existing original excerpts; retrieval runs locally without a model server.

The nine lenses and two bases are packaged ahead of time. Custom axes and adjustable basis construction remain available in the local research app.

Reading the map

The flat map uses t-SNE fitted to 5,000 fixed tracks in the selected search space. Other tracks are placed by interpolation from eight sample neighbours. Those positions are approximate: screen distance is not the retrieval score. The map stays stable as you change songs and reorganizes as you change lens or basis.

Colours show caption- and metadata-defined profiles, separately from the geometric layout and nearest-neighbour scores. The same profile keeps its meaning across text and Adam bases; its displayed distribution moves with the basis. The ten exact nearest neighbours are larger dots at full opacity, connected to the starting-song star by fine lines. Those links follow the dots as the map moves.

Groups with shared attributes

Instrument profiles are combinations of explicitly named instruments: electric guitar + bass + drums, bass + drums + synths, or acoustic guitar + piano, for example. We extract positive instrument mentions with negation checks, discover recurring combinations on one half of the corpus, and require them to recur on the other half. Frequent trios are considered first, followed by pairs and sparse/solo profiles, with at most eight total groups, including the other/unspecified pool. Every caption assigned to a combination must mention all its components; additional instruments are allowed. Tracks can match several combinations, so a fixed, reproducible priority assigns each track to one displayed group. Missing evidence stays neutral instead of being assigned to the nearest prototype.

Every category has at most eight groups in total, including metadata fallbacks and other/unspecified cases. Vocal, mood, groove and production profiles similarly combine explicit caption attributes. Vocal/instrumental metadata takes precedence over contradictory caption wording; vocal tracks without a stated delivery are shown as delivery unspecified. Groove profiles use explicit rhythmic descriptions where available, then dataset danceability bands for the remaining tracks. Language uses seven spoken-language metadata groups—English, Portuguese, Spanish, Korean, French, Japanese and German—plus an other/no-language bucket that includes the dataset’s instrumental marker. These are supplied language tags, not language detection from the audio; the instrumental marker may disagree with the separate vocal/instrumental field. The language basis includes 36 descriptions across 12 languages and four controls for instrumental, wordless, multilingual and spoken material. Speed uses the dataset’s slow/medium/fast labels. Energy uses five fixed intervals of its metadata score, from very low to very high; click a group to inspect the interval. Genre uses 58 style descriptions including fusion and experimental controls, with seven broad display families from the greatest number of matching recorded genre tags; ties use a fixed family order. Unmatched tags stay other/unspecified. Genre labels describe dataset tags, not independently verified audio classes. Full-space groups use genre metadata.

These are partitions of the recorded caption/metadata evidence, not independently verified audio classes. Recurrence in both corpus halves confirms that a profile is supported by enough examples; it is not an accuracy measurement. The packaged page groups all 109,269 tracks. The local app learns profiles from its 5,000 display sample and applies those same rules to new query/results; custom lenses retain geometric groups.

The optional overlay shows soft Gaussian density fields for these actual group members in the 2D projection. Labels choose dense parts of the distribution and avoid overlapping. Click one for its group size and matching caption phrases. Settings contains the complete group list, including profiles whose labels cannot fit in the current viewport. Colors and group labels never change the lens basis or the ten nearest neighbours.

Zoom closer to watch every on-frame track resolve into a frozen waveform of its actual 30-second clip. Dense areas use smaller envelopes; all waveforms are drawn, while title/artist labels and controls avoid overlaps. Click or tap a waveform to play or pause it; only the playing waveform animates, synchronized with the top player. Info opens its glass card and the star chooses a new starting song. On mobile, long press opens details without playing or moving the seed. Scroll or use the corner controls to zoom; on mobile, pinch with two fingers and move them to pan. Cards open on hover, focus or tap. A separate star button in the preview makes a track the starting song; tapping a dot or its title never changes the seed. Dots are redrawn at native display resolution at every zoom level.

Listening

The compact glass player shows the current track and artist, with a rounded waveform in the track’s map colour. The waveform responds to live audio samples from the playing clip, and stops moving when playback pauses. The playing track also pulses on the map. The public page streams the original 30-second Music4All excerpt from our Hugging Face audio service, without Spotify. The service reads a private dataset on the server; individual clips are publicly playable and no dataset credential is sent to the browser.

An optional Spotify configuration offers a fallback: open the track in Spotify, or choose a visible in-page player using Spotify’s supported iframe controls. The widget is loaded only when requested and stays visible during playback. Spotify determines playback availability for the listener. Music4All captions describe the dataset clip; a Spotify preview may play a different passage of the track. About Spotify embeds.

Caption highlights and similarity attributes are model estimates, not verified annotations or causal explanations. An attribute is shown when its original text embedding has high affinity to both the starting song and its neighbour.
